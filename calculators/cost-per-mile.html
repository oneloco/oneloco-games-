How the math worksThe formulas, the three kinds of cost, and a worked example

Take everything the truck cost over a period and divide it by every mile it ran in that same period. Then compare that to what loads actually pay.

Cost per mile      = total cost ÷ all miles driven
Earned per mile    = rate per loaded mile × (1 − empty mile share)
Profit per mile    = earned per mile − cost per mile
Break-even rate    = cost per mile ÷ (1 − empty mile share)
Fixed costsInsurance, truck and trailer notes, permits, IFTA and IRP, ELD and dispatch. You pay them whether the wheels turn or not, so more miles make each mile cheaper.
Variable costsFuel, repairs, tires, tolls, driver pay, factoring. They rise with every mile you run, so running more doesn't bring them down per mile.
Empty milesDeadhead burns the same fuel and tires as a loaded mile, but nobody pays for it. It quietly lowers what you earn per mile driven.

Worked example (made-up numbers): a month of 10,000 miles costs $16,500, so the truck costs $1.65 a mile. With 15% of miles empty, a $2.10 load rate earns about $1.79 for every mile driven, which leaves 14 cents a mile, or roughly $1,350 for the month. Below $1.94 per loaded mile, the truck loses money.

Common questions8 quick answers
What is cost per mile?

It's your total operating cost for a period divided by all the miles the truck ran in that period. It tells you what a mile costs before you look at what a load pays.

How is CPM different from RPM?

RPM is the rate a load pays per mile. CPM is what the truck costs you per mile. The gap between them is your profit, and a load with a strong RPM can still lose money if you don't know your CPM.

Do empty miles count?

Yes. They cost fuel, tires and time like any other mile, so they're included in cost per mile. Rate is entered per loaded mile, and the calculator adjusts for the share of miles you run empty.

Should I put my own pay in driver pay?

No, if you drive your own truck. Leave it out, and the profit line is what's left for you. Put in driver pay only for drivers you pay.

Week, month or year?

Use whichever matches your records. Weekly settlements, monthly statements and yearly tax numbers all give the same cost per mile, as long as every box uses the same period.

Where does insurance fit in?

Insurance is a fixed cost. Your premium doesn't change with how far you drive in a given week, so more miles spread it thinner. Coverage, limits, deductibles and how you pay all change what it costs you per mile.

What does the break-even rate mean?

It's the lowest rate per loaded mile that covers all the costs you entered, after accounting for empty miles. Loads below it keep the truck busy but cost you money.

Does this save what I type?

No. The math runs in your browser, and nothing is stored on a server or sent to anyone.
